## Cookies and Privacy

### What are cookies?
A cookie is a small text file that a website saves on your computer when you visit 
it. Think of it like a sticky note the website leaves in your browser so it can 
"remember" you the next time you visit — like staying logged in, or remembering 
items in your shopping cart.

### First-Party Cookies
Created by the website you're actually visiting (e.g., novapay.com saving your 
login session). These are generally normal and expected.

### Third-Party Cookies
Created by a *different* website than the one you're visiting, usually placed by 
ads or embedded content (like a Facebook "Like" button or an ad banner). These are 
the ones that can track you *across multiple websites*, not just the one you're on. 
This is exactly what was found on NovaPay's computers in Event 4.

### Session Cookies
Temporary: they only last while your browser is open. Once you close the browser, 
they're deleted. Example: staying logged in while you browse, but getting logged 
out after closing the tab.

### Persistent Cookies
These stick around even after you close your browser, sometimes for weeks, months, 
or years until they expire or you delete them. Example: a site remembering your 
language preference every time you return.

### Authentication Cookies
Store your login session so you don't have to re-enter your password on every page. 
These are important for security — if stolen, an attacker could access your account 
without needing your password at all (this is called "session hijacking").

### Tracking Cookies
Usually third-party cookies specifically designed to follow your browsing activity 
across many different websites, building a profile of your interests — mainly used 
for targeted advertising.

### Privacy Risks
- Advertisers/data brokers can build a detailed profile of an employee's browsing 
  habits without them realizing it
- Sensitive browsing patterns (health sites, financial sites, job searches) could be 
  exposed to third parties
- Stolen authentication cookies can let an attacker access an account without a 
  password
- Large numbers of unmanaged third-party cookies (as found in Event 4) suggest weak 
  browser privacy controls across the company

---

## Are All Cookies Dangerous?

**No — most cookies are not dangerous.**

Cookies are just small text files; they can't run programs, spread viruses, or 
infect your computer like malware does. Most cookies exist simply to make browsing 
convenient — staying logged in, remembering your cart, remembering your settings.

The **real risk isn't the cookie itself, it's what it's used for and who has 
access to it**:
- **First-party, session, and authentication cookies** are generally safe and 
  necessary for websites to work properly.
- **Third-party and tracking cookies** aren't "dangerous" in a technical sense, but 
  they raise **privacy concerns**  they follow you around the web without clearly 
  asking permission.
- The only real security risk is if an **authentication cookie is stolen** (e.g., 
  through malware or an unsecured network) then an attacker could hijack your 
  logged-in session.
